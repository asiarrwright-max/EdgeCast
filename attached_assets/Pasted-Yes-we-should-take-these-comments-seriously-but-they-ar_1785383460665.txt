Yes, we should take these comments seriously—but they are reasons to audit EdgeCast carefully, not reasons to abandon it.

The biggest thing that stands out is this combination from your dashboard:

* Average claimed edge: 38.5 percentage points
* Confidence: High
* Actual win rate: 15%
* ROI: –78.3%

Those numbers do not fit together. A system finding truly enormous 38-point pricing errors should not lose 85% of its settled trades. That suggests at least one of two things:

1. EdgeCast’s probability model is dramatically wrong, or
2. Something in the market interpretation, settlement, pricing, or paper-trade accounting is wrong.

Before waiting weeks for “training,” I would verify the second possibility.

What the comments mean for EdgeCast

“Backend P&L or decimal formatting could be wrong”

This is possible, although a display-formatting problem would usually make the ROI dollar amount wrong, not turn wins into losses.

Replit should confirm:

* Kalshi prices are interpreted consistently as cents versus decimals.
* A price of 35 means $0.35, not $35 or 0.35 cents.
* Contract quantities and payouts are calculated correctly.
* YES and NO costs use the appropriate side’s actual price.
* Profit for a winning contract is calculated from the entry cost, not from a generic $1 amount incorrectly.

Important—but it probably does not fully explain the 15% win rate, because win/loss classification is separate from P&L.

“Phantom trades”

This is extremely relevant.

A paper trader can pretend it purchased something at the displayed price even when a real order could not have filled there. For example:

* The app sees a YES offer at 30¢.
* There is only one contract available at 30¢.
* The simulator records ten contracts at 30¢.
* In reality, the remaining contracts would cost more—or the market might move before the order executes.

EdgeCast should store:

* Actual bid and ask at the decision time.
* Which price it assumes it would pay.
* Available quantity at that price.
* Timestamp of the quote.
* Simulated fill quantity.
* Any assumed slippage.

However, phantom fills normally make paper results look too good. EdgeCast is doing badly, so this is essential before real money but probably is not causing the current severe losses.

“Settlement logic”

This is the concern I would investigate first.

Weather contracts have exact rules involving:

* The official reporting station.
* The exact measurement.
* Date and time zone.
* Whether the event is “above,” “at least,” “between,” or “below.”
* Rounding conventions.
* The official source Kalshi uses.

A forecast for “Chicago” might not correspond exactly to the airport station used by the contract. A prediction of 90°F does not necessarily win a contract requiring “above 90°F.” Rain at one station does not settle a contract based on another station.

A single parsing mistake could systematically reverse or misclassify trades.

Replit should manually trace at least 20 settled trades from beginning to end:

Market wording → station → threshold → EdgeCast probability → selected side → entry price → official result → Kalshi outcome → calculated P&L.

Not merely review the code. Compare each field against the actual contract.

“Fees and slippage”

Yes, these must eventually be included.

A paper system can appear profitable before:

* Trading fees.
* Bid-ask spread.
* Slippage.
* Limited liquidity.
* Orders that never fill.

But fees and slippage are not the main explanation for –78% ROI. They can turn a small apparent advantage into a loss; they usually do not turn a genuinely strong system into this result.

“Backtesting is flawed”

The commenter is broadly right. Historical tests can accidentally cheat by using information that would not have been available when the trade was placed. This is called look-ahead bias.

Examples for EdgeCast would include:

* Using a forecast revision published after the hypothetical entry time.
* Using the final daily high while pretending it was a prediction input.
* Comparing against a historical market price that was not available at that timestamp.
* Selecting only historical markets that later had complete, convenient data.
* Tuning thresholds repeatedly against the same test results.

This matters if EdgeCast preloads data or runs historical backtests. The current forward paper trading is valuable precisely because it avoids much of that cheating—as long as every prediction and price is timestamped and permanently preserved.

“Anyone with an edge won’t share it” and “strategies collapse with volume”

There is some truth, but it is not especially threatening to your project.

Weather markets may have limited liquidity, so a strategy that works with $20 trades might not support $50,000 trades. But you are not currently trying to deploy institutional-scale money. Capacity can be measured later.

And a person claiming “I make $450 per day” without records is not evidence that their strategy works. Treat the comment as an anecdote, not validation.

My honest assessment

I would pause the assumption that V2 merely needs more training until the system passes a technical audit.

Twenty trades is far too few to judge profitability, but three wins and seventeen losses alongside “high confidence” and 38.5pp average edge is unusual enough to investigate now. Waiting for 500 trades will not help if the model is reading the contract backward, matching the wrong station, mishandling thresholds, or using the wrong price.

I would keep paper trading running, but ask Replit to conduct a read-only paper-trading realism and settlement audit. Do not let it rewrite the prediction engine during that audit.
